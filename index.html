Owl — Noise Reduction

Serrated leading-edge feathers break up turbulence before it becomes sound.

Nature

Owl wing feathers have a comb-like serrated leading edge and a soft, fringed trailing edge, plus a velvety upper surface.

Engineering principle

The serrations break large turbulent structures into many smaller ones, spreading noise energy across frequencies the ear barely registers.

Application

Serrated wind-turbine blade edges and fan blades cut aerodynamic noise by several decibels without a major efficiency penalty.

Humpback Whale — Aerodynamic Control

Bumps on the flipper's leading edge keep airflow attached longer.

Smooth edgeTubercled edge
↔

Drag to compare: a smooth edge lets flow separate early and stall; tubercles keep the airflow attached at steeper angles.

Nature

Humpback flippers have rounded bumps, tubercles, spaced along the leading edge, unlike the smooth edges of most fins.

Engineering principle

Tubercles create small counter-rotating vortices that re-energize the boundary layer, delaying flow separation to a much higher angle.

Application

Tubercled leading edges on wind-turbine and fan blades allow steeper angles before stall, improving control and reducing drag.

Bird Wing — Efficient Flight

Curvature over the top of the wing is the basis of lift generation.

Flat / low camberCambered wing
↔

Drag to compare: a flat, low-camber section produces little lift; the curved cambered wing speeds up flow on top and generates lift.

Nature

Bird wings are cambered: curved on top, flatter beneath, with a rounded leading edge and tapering trailing edge.

Engineering principle

Air travelling over the curved top speeds up and drops in pressure relative to the underside, producing a net upward force: lift, alongside drag from friction and pressure.

Application

Aircraft wing airfoils use the same cambered cross-section, tuned in wind tunnels to balance lift generated against drag incurred.

Summary

Transportation: three separate lessons, one goal

Serrated edges quiet the wing, tubercles keep flow attached at steep angles, and camber generates lift efficiently — together informing quieter, more efficient aircraft.

02 · The engineering problem

Cooling a building without burning energy

Termite mounds regulate internal temperature within a narrow band, in harsh climates, using no mechanical parts — just geometry and airflow.

Warm air rising from the core
Cool air drawn in through openings
Surface openings

The mound's tunnels act like a chimney: heat from the colony's metabolism warms air at the core, which rises and exits through upper openings, drawing cooler air in through lower openings — continuous passive ventilation, no fans required.

03 · Applied example

CH2 — Council House 2, Melbourne

One of the first office buildings to directly borrow termite-mound ventilation logic, alongside solar shading and water-based cooling.

What each part borrows from the mound

Ventilation shaft & rooftop turbines

Vertical shafts and wind-driven turbines pull warm air up and out, the same stack effect the mound uses.

Solar shading louvers

Angled screens on the sun-facing side reduce direct heat gain, similar to how a mound's shape and orientation limit direct sun exposure on the brood chamber.

Water cascades & exposed thermal mass

Evaporative cooling and dense concrete slabs damp temperature swings, echoing the mound's thick, moisture-regulating walls.

Combined, CH2 relies far less on mechanical air conditioning than a conventional office tower of the same size.
